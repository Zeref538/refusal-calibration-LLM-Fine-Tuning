Refusal Calibration

Ask a small AI something it can't know and it makes up an answer, stated with total confidence. Teaching it to say "I don't know" fixes that, and quietly creates a second problem: now it says "I don't know" to things it knew. I measured both mistakes at once, across 19 versions of the model.

LoRA fine-tuning Qwen2.5 (1.5B and 3B) for calibrated abstention, scored on both error directions over one frozen 800-item eval. 19 arms, paired bootstrap CIs, and a seed noise floor that turns out to swamp the headline curve at 1.5B.

01The result

It worked. Look what it cost.

The four numbers, always together

All four tiles describe the same model, the 1.5B reference version. The bar under each number is its margin of error. This page never prints a number without one.

The number I could have led with, and why I didn't

Why the single number is dishonest

"Made-up answers cut by 92%" is completely true, and it would have been the impressive headline. It's also close to meaningless on its own: the same model now refuses 61.5% of the questions it used to get right, and its score on questions it can answer fell from 100% to 29%. That's not a model that got better. It's a model that got quieter.

"Hallucinations −92.5 pp [−95.0, −90.0]" is true and publishable. It is also nearly worthless alone: the same checkpoint now over-refuses 61.5% [54.9, 68.4] of base-correct items, with accuracy down 71.0 pp. Reporting one axis without the other describes a model that got quieter, not better.

Why the untrained model scores 100%: the "answerable" category was defined as the questions it already got right. That's deliberate. It's what makes "it refused something it knew" measurable at all.

Base accuracy is 100% by construction: the answerable class is defined as base-correct under k=16 sampling, which is precisely what makes over-refusal measurable against it.

02The problem

Two ways to be wrong, and everyone only reports one

Why "just train it to refuse" is a trap

Picture two new hires. The first never says "I'm not sure." Every answer sounds authoritative, and some of them are invented. The second says "I'm not sure" to everything, including things they know perfectly well. Both are useless, in opposite directions. You want the one who can tell the difference.

A small AI model out of the box is the first hire. On questions it had no way of answering, it gave a confident wrong answer 95.5% of the time. Training it to refuse turns it into the second hire. Nearly every published "we fixed hallucinations" result reports only the first number going down, and stays quiet about the second going up.

Why it's called calibration

A well-calibrated instrument is one you can trust in both directions: it doesn't read high, and it doesn't read low. Same idea here. The goal was never "refuse more." It was "refuse in exactly the right places."

Most published hallucination fixes report one number: the hallucination rate went down. They rarely report what happened to the questions the model could answer. A model that abstains on everything scores perfectly on hallucination and is useless in production.

So the deliverable here is a curve, not a point, and, as it turned out, a noise floor underneath that curve. Over-refusal is measured specifically against base-correct items, which separates "the fine-tune became cowardly" from "the model never knew that anyway."

03How it was built

Five steps, run on a free graphics card

Teach the model its own boundary; don't guess it

  1. Find out what it actually knows

    Self-knowledge labeling (k=16)

    You can't teach a model its limits until you know where they are. So every one of 8,000 trivia questions was put to the model 16 separate times, and its answers checked against the real ones. Gets it right nearly every time? That's something it knows. Never gets it right? That's beyond it. The boundary is the model's own, not one I assumed for it.

    Sample the base model k=16 per question over 8,000 TriviaQA items (128,000 generations), scoring against gold alias sets. Consistently correct → answerable; near-zero → unknown; deliberately broken questions → defective. Labels are empirical, not assumed.

  2. Seal an exam before any studying happens

    Freeze the eval, enforce it with a lock

    800 questions were set aside as the test (200 answerable, 200 impossible, 200 borderline, 200 with something wrong in the question itself) and locked before a single training run started. Every version of the model afterwards sat the identical exam. Without this, you're grading a student on questions they already studied.

    800 items, stratified 200/200/200/200 across answerable, unknown, borderline and defective, frozen ahead of training and enforced by a lock file so no later session can leak into the reported numbers.

  3. Agree on one way to say "I don't know"

    One structured refusal contract

    A refusal isn't useful if it's just an apology. The model was taught to answer in a fixed form that also names why it's declining: the question is unanswerable, or it's about events after its cutoff, or it contains a false claim, or it's too vague. Now software downstream can act on the refusal, and I can grade whether it refused for the right reason.

    Every arm emits {"answer": …, "reason": …}, reason drawn from a closed set: unknown, after_cutoff, false_premise, missing_context, ambiguous. Structured so a planner can branch on it, and so abstention quality is mechanically gradeable. The parser also accepts plain-English refusals, so baselines aren't scored as hallucinating when they politely decline.

  4. Train 15 versions, changing one thing at a time

    15 LoRA fine-tunes, one axis per run

    Retraining a whole model is expensive, so each version got a small bolt-on adjustment instead, about 1% the size of the model. Then: five versions varying how much refusal practice they got (from 10% up to 90% of the examples), two identical repeats to check for randomness, and the rest changing exactly one setting each, including a bigger model run twice. Changing one thing per run is the only way to know what caused what.

    LoRA adapters (18.5M trainable params, 1.2% of the 1.5B base). Five mix ratios spanning 10–90% abstain, two seed replicates of the reference, dosage (epochs), capacity (rank, LR), a reason-ablation, and a 3B scale check with its own seed replicate. All 17 configs generated from one RUNS table, so "exactly one thing changed" is structural, not manual.

  5. Grade every version on both mistakes

    Score both directions, paired, with intervals

    Each version sat the sealed exam, and every result was reported with a margin of error, the same idea as a poll saying "42%, plus or minus 3." Without that, a difference of three points and a difference of thirty look identical on a slide.

    Paired bootstrap CIs on the difference between each arm and its matched base, so a 3-point gap and an 18-point gap are never conflated. 3B arms are scored only against 3B baselines.

05The noise floor

I trained the exact same thing three times and got three different answers

The seed noise floor swamps the curve

Training has a random element: the order examples are shuffled into. Three runs, the same recipe, the same data, the same settings; only that shuffle differed. If the method were stable, all three should land in roughly the same place.

v3, v12 and v13 are the identical reference recipe under seeds 0, 1 and 2. Every other variable is held.

These two extra runs demolished my own headline chart

The replicates invalidated the curve they were built to support

Pure luck moves the refusal rate by 18.5 points. Most of the differences between the five models in the chart above are smaller than that. So "50% refusal practice beats 25%" is not something this experiment can claim. That gap is indistinguishable from the dice. Only the two extreme ends separate for real.

Seed alone moves over-refusal 18.5 pp and hallucination 10.8 pp. Most between-arm gaps in v1–v5 are smaller. The ranking of 25 vs. 50 vs. 75% abstain is therefore unresolvable at this scale; only the endpoints separate cleanly.

Those two repeat runs were in the plan before any result existed, precisely because they could do this. They produced no exciting number of their own. They are the reason the other runs can be trusted at all. Cost: 2.4 hours of GPU time, about 13% of the training budget. That is the cheapest insurance in this entire project.

v12 and v13 were scheduled ahead of any result for exactly this purpose. Without a noise floor, "mix A beats mix B by 4 points" is unfalsifiable and the whole curve rests on gaps of that size. Cost: 2.4 GPU-hours, ~13% of the training budget.

This is the one that actually worked

At 3B, abstention stops being a trade

The bigger model cut its made-up answers roughly in half, refused wrongly only 9.6% of the time instead of 61.5%, and got better at the questions it could answer rather than worse. It also named the right reason for refusing 65% of the time, up from 11%. So the small model's dismal result isn't "this technique doesn't work." It's that the small model didn't have the room.

−5.5 pp hallucination [−8.5, −2.5] while gaining 4.0 pp accuracy [−4.4, +12.4], at 9.6 pp over-refusal versus 61.5 pp at 1.5B. Abstention quality 11.1% → 64.6%, and calibration held at base level (ECE 8.4% → 7.3%), the only fine-tuned arm to manage that. The 1.5B result is a capacity floor, not a refutation of the method. Both claims are re-tested against a second seed below, and one of them does not fully survive.

It held, and the stability is itself the finding

Replicated, with the 3B seed spread 4–7× tighter

The second run lands in the same place. Where the small model swung 18.5 points on wrongful refusals between seeds, the bigger model moves 2.4. Seed 1 independently beat its own untrained baseline on accuracy too (49.5% against 41.5%), by a wider margin than the first run managed. So the wild instability at 1.5B isn't a property of this training recipe. It's a property of being too small.

Seed spread at 3B is 2.4–4.0 pp on the headline axes versus 10.8–18.5 pp at 1.5B. Both seeds hold over-refusal in single digits and both gain accuracy over base_3b. The instability is a capacity artifact, not a recipe artifact, which strengthens the capacity-floor reading rather than merely failing to refute it.

Two honest caveats. The drop in made-up answers was clear-cut on the first run but not on the second: seed 1's margin of error just includes "no change," so that specific claim rests on one of the two runs. And the confidence-error result did move: 7.3% on one seed, 11.8% on the other, so "calibration survived" is weaker than it first looked. Both are exactly what a replicate is for.

Two qualifications. Seed 1's hallucination delta is −2.7 pp [−5.8, +0.3], the same direction as seed 0's −5.5 pp [−8.5, −2.5] but the CI crosses zero, so the hallucination reduction is significant in one of two runs. ECE is the least stable axis (7.3% vs 11.8% against a base of 8.4%), so "calibration did not degrade" is downgraded to "did not degrade on one seed, mildly on the other." Over-refusal, abstention quality and accuracy all replicate cleanly.

07The cheap control

Could you skip all this and just ask it nicely?

Prompt-only control

Worth checking before spending 30 hours of computing: what if you simply instruct the model to admit uncertainty, with no training at all?

The zero-cost control: an abstention instruction in the system prompt, untrained weights.

VersionMade-upWrong refusals ScoreRight reasonConfidence error

Asking nicely does help a bit: made-up answers fell about 16 points. But it also cost 19 points of accuracy, and made the model's stated confidence far less trustworthy. On the bigger model it made things worse. And notice the "right reason" column: 0%. Prompted models decline in vague prose without ever naming a usable reason.

Prompting buys a real −16.5 pp [−21.4, −11.8] on hallucination at 1.5B, at a cost of 19 pp accuracy and a near-tripled ECE. At 3B it raises hallucination and blows ECE out to 47.0%. Abstention quality is 0.0% for both prompt arms: prose declines that never emit a valid reason code.

09Confidence

When it says it's 90% sure, is it right 90% of the time?

Expected calibration error by arm

A model that says "90% confident" and is right 90% of the time is trustworthy even when wrong, because you can plan around it. This chart shows how far each version's stated confidence drifts from reality. Shorter is better. The dashed line is the untrained model.

ECE per arm; the dashed line is the untrained 1.5B base at 7.3%.

Untrained & prompt-only Small-model fine-tunes Bigger model (3B)

Almost every trained small model came out less trustworthy about its own confidence than the model it started from, an easy cost to miss if you only track wrong answers. The bigger model held its ground on its first run (7.3%) and slipped a little on its second (11.8%). The two worst bars are the prompt-only versions: told to express uncertainty, they express it almost at random.

Every 1.5B fine-tune degraded calibration relative to base (+7 to +9 pp, CIs excluding zero). Only v14 at 3B matched base-level ECE while also abstaining well; its seed-1 replicate landed at 11.8%. Prompt-only arms are worst by a wide margin, 47.0% at 3B, i.e. stated confidence that actively misleads.

What the four categories mean

Answerable: the model demonstrably knows this. Unknown: it doesn't, and no amount of thinking will help. Borderline: it gets this right sometimes, the hardest case to handle well. Defective: the question itself is broken. It assumes something false, leaves out what you'd need, or is too vague to have one answer. A good model spots the difference and says which.

12Engineering

The hard part wasn't the AI

The parts that weren't the model

Most of the work went into making a 30-hour experiment survive a free service that shuts the machine off every 9 hours.

Most of the effort was making a 30-GPU-hour experiment survive a 9-hour session cap.

  • Nothing finished is ever lost. Every stage saves as it goes and picks up where it died, mid-question or mid-run. A half-written result is renamed into place only once it's complete, so a partial run can never be marked as a real one. Every stage writes as it runs and resumes from the exact item it died on; partial output lands in responses.partial and is atomically renamed only when complete, so a truncated arm can never be scored as whole.
  • A crash can't take the session with it. One failed run doesn't destroy the ones that already worked. "Run everything again" is always the right button, however many attempts it takes. Failures are contained per stage; the rest of the notebook, including the save cell, still runs. Finished work is skipped on rerun.
  • The exam is sealed with a lock file, so no later run can quietly contaminate the numbers being reported.
  • One table generates every configuration, so "only one thing changed between these two runs" is guaranteed by the code, not by my memory. 17 YAMLs from one RUNS list, making single-variable isolation structural rather than manual.
  • Marking runs on any laptop from the saved answers, with no graphics card. That is what rescued this page when the final session returned an empty log. with CPU-only scoring, reproducible from saved generations, which recovered all 19 arms when the kernel log was unreadable.
  • Cheap preview runs. A step limit gives a 3-minute look at where a run is heading before committing an hour of a 9-hour window to it. A max_steps cap yields ~3-minute directional previews before spending a full hour of session budget.
13Takeaways

Four things I'd do again

Takeaways

  • Budget for repeat runs before extra experiments. Two runs that produced no new headline, 13% of the training budget, were the difference between a real finding and a convincing-looking artifact. Seed replicates at 13% of the training budget were the difference between a curve and a plausible artifact.
  • Report the axis that makes the result look bad. The 92% improvement and the 61.5% wrongful-refusal rate are the same model. −92.5 pp hallucination and +61.5 pp over-refusal are the same checkpoint.
  • Try the free version first. Just asking the model nicely got a real chunk of the improvement for zero compute, and cost 19 points of accuracy, which only a two-sided test would ever reveal. The prompt-only control captured 16.5 pp of the hallucination win at zero GPU cost, at −19 pp accuracy, visible only on a two-axis eval.
  • Model size can be the whole story. The small-model conclusion ("this trade isn't worth it") flips on the bigger one. Testing a single size would have published the wrong general claim. The 1.5B conclusion reverses at 3B; a single-size study would have generalised the wrong finding.

Two ways to be wrong. Measured both.
