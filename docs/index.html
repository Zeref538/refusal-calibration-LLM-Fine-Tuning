Case study — LLM fine-tuning & evaluation

Teaching a small model to say “I don’t know”
and finding out what it costs

Small language models confabulate — confidently, fluently, wrong. The obvious fix (train it to refuse) creates a quieter failure: over-refusal, where the model declines things it actually knew. This project measured both error directions on one frozen eval across 18 arms. The fix works — and at 1.5B it costs more than it saves.

This is why the single number is dishonest

“Hallucinations down 92%” is true and would have been the publishable headline. It is also nearly worthless on its own: the same model now refuses 61.5% of the questions its own base answered correctly. Reporting one axis without the other describes a model that got quieter, not better.

Base accuracy reads 100% by construction: the answerable class is defined as items the base model got right under k=16 sampling, which is exactly what makes over-refusal measurable against it.

The problem

Why "just train it to refuse" is a trap

Most published hallucination fixes report one number: the hallucination rate went down. They rarely report what happened to the questions the model could answer. A model that abstains on everything scores perfectly on hallucination and is useless in production. The honest deliverable is a curve, not a point — and, as it turned out here, a noise floor underneath that curve.

Method

Teach the model its own boundary, don't guess it

  1. Self-knowledge labeling

    Sample the base model k=16 times per question across 8,000 TriviaQA questions (128,000 generations) and check each against gold aliases, rather than assuming what the model "should" know. Consistently correct → answerable; near-zero → unknown. Deliberately broken questions → defective. The model is taught its own boundary, not someone else's.

  2. A frozen, held-out eval

    800 items, exactly 200 each of answerable / unknown / borderline / defective, frozen before any training run touched it and enforced with a lock file so no later session could leak into it.

  3. One structured contract for refusal

    Every arm emits {"answer": …, "reason": …} with the reason drawn from a closed set — unknown, after_cutoff, false_premise, missing_context, ambiguous. Structured, not prose, so a downstream planner can branch on it and so "was the refusal for the right reason?" becomes mechanically gradeable.

  4. 14 LoRA fine-tunes, one axis changed per run

    A curve across the training mix (10 → 90% abstain examples), two seed replicates of the reference recipe, dosage (epochs), capacity (LoRA rank and LR), a reason-ablation, and a 3B scale check — each isolating exactly one variable against a shared reference config generated from one table.

  5. Both error directions, always paired

    Every arm scored on the identical frozen eval with bootstrap confidence intervals on the paired difference, and 3B arms scored only against 3B baselines — comparing a 3B fine-tune to a 1.5B base would be meaningless.

The seed replicates invalidated the curve they were built to support

Changing only the random seed moves over-refusal by 18.5 points and hallucination by 10.8. Most of the gaps between the mix arms are smaller than that. So the ranking of 25% vs. 50% vs. 75% abstain training is not resolvable at this scale — reading a "best mix" off the curve would be reading noise. Only the extremes (v1 at 10% vs. v5 at 90%) separate cleanly.

This is the entire reason v12 and v13 were in the run plan before any result existed. Two runs that produce no new headline are what makes the other twelve interpretable — without them, "mix A beats mix B by 4 points" is unfalsifiable, and the whole curve rests on gaps of exactly that size. The cost of that honesty was 2.4 GPU-hours, about 13% of the training budget.

The tradeoff nearly vanishes

At 3B, abstention stops being a trade

The 3B fine-tune cut hallucination by 5.5 pp [−8.5, −2.5] while gaining 4.0 pp of accuracy [−4.4, +12.4] and paying only 9.6 pp of over-refusal — versus 61.5 pp at 1.5B. Abstention quality went from 11.1% to 64.6%, and it is the only fine-tuned arm whose calibration improved (ECE 8.4% → 7.3%). The 1.5B result is not "refusal training doesn't work" — it is a capacity floor.

Calibration

Expected Calibration Error — is the stated confidence worth anything?

ECE by arm (lower is better)

Every 1.5B fine-tune came out less calibrated than the base it started from

The 1.5B base is the best-calibrated 1.5B arm at 7.3% — abstention training reliably degraded it (+7 to +9 pp, CIs excluding zero). Only v14 at 3B matched base-level calibration (7.3%) while also abstaining well. Prompt-only arms are the worst on this axis by a wide margin: 47.0% at 3B, i.e. stated confidence that is actively misleading.

Engineering notes

The parts that weren't the model

Most of the real effort was making a 30-GPU-hour experiment survive a free tier that kills the machine every 9 hours:

  • Every stage is resumable. The probe skips finished questions, training skips runs that already have an adapter, and generation resumes an arm from the exact item it died on — writing to responses.partial and atomically renaming only when complete, so a half-written arm can never be scored as if it were whole.
  • Failures are contained to their stage. A crashed run doesn't take the session with it; everything already on disk survives and the notebook continues to its zip-and-save cell. "Run All" is always the correct button, however many times it takes.
  • The eval is frozen and lock-enforced, so no later training session can quietly leak into the numbers being reported.
  • One table generates every config. 17 YAML files come from one RUNS list, so "exactly one thing changed per run" is structurally guaranteed rather than manually maintained.
  • Scoring is CPU-only and reproducible off saved generations — which is what saved this write-up when the final Kaggle session returned a 0-byte log: all 18 arms were re-scored locally from the downloaded outputs in about 25 minutes.
  • Budgeted preview runs. A max_steps cap gives a ~3-minute look at a run's direction before committing a full hour of a 9-hour session to it.

What I'd tell someone starting this

  • Budget for seed replicates before you budget for arms. Two extra runs (13% of the training budget) were the difference between a curve and a plausible-looking artifact.
  • Report the axis that makes your result look bad. The 92% hallucination drop and the 61.5% over-refusal are the same model.
  • Check the free baseline. Prompting got a real 16.5 pp of the hallucination win for zero GPU-hours — and cost 19 pp of accuracy, which is the kind of thing only a two-axis eval reveals.
  • Scale can be the variable. The 1.5B conclusion ("abstention training is a bad trade") reverses at 3B. A single-size study would have published the wrong general claim.
